Drainage Inspector

A field app for engineers walking a railway under construction, checking what has actually been built against what the drawings say should be there. Nine sub-sections, more than 380 km, two lines. This brief is mostly domain — because the hard part of this design is working out how to represent drainage, and you cannot do that without first understanding how drainage sits on a railway and how the drawings depict it.

01The brief

Design a field app, from scratch, for verifying railway drainage against its design. Interaction model, information architecture and visual language are all yours.

The app answers one question, asked repeatedly, while standing on a railway embankment in northern Nigeria: what is supposed to be here, and is it built?

The drainage design exists as hundreds of large-format drawings and a design report per sub-section. On site there is no signal, no shade, and no practical way to carry paper. An engineer needs to know, at whatever position he is standing at, which ditches and culverts the design puts there, on which side, of what type — then record what he can actually see, and have that record survive back to the office.

It is used by the consultant supervising the contractor, not by the contractor. That distinction shapes everything: the app is an instrument of verification, and its output ends up in documents sent to the party being checked. A wrong number in this app becomes a wrong number in a formal letter.

The one-line version

A single-purpose instrument for comparing design intent against physical reality at a known position along a line, built for one hand, bright sun and no network.

What varies between sections

This is the part that will shape your data model and your navigation more than anything else.

  • Character. One section is mostly open country on low embankment. Another opens with a freight yard and runs through three stations, each with buried collector drains that do not exist on open line. Terrain, cut-versus-fill, and watercourse crossings all change the drainage mix completely.
  • Size. One known section is 41.6 km with 30 plan sheets; the next is 63 km with 47 sheets and 119 separate culvert drawings.
  • Construction state. Some sections are largely built, some barely started, some have work that began two years ago and stopped.
  • Document state. Drawings are revised independently — one section currently has sheets at revisions 04, 05, 06 and 07 simultaneously. Each drawing carries its own approval status, and some are approved subject to comments while others are not cleared to build at all.
  • Completeness of the data. Sections will be added over time. The app must be usable when only two of nine exist, and still make sense at nine.
Chainage alone does not identify a place

There are two lines, and both begin at Kano. A given chainage may therefore exist twice across the project. Position is line + chainage + side, never chainage on its own. Confirm the branch’s chainage datum before relying on it, but design as though collision is possible.

03Who uses it

One primary user type. Two secondary ones who never open it but consume what comes out.

Primary — the site engineer

Junior civil engineer, drainage supervision, employed by the consultant. Walks or drives stretches of the alignment on half-day trips — out in the morning, back before or shortly after lunch, then office time to reconcile findings and write them up.

On site he is holding a phone in one hand and often a tape, a camera or a drawing roll in the other. He is frequently walking. He is checking dozens of features per kilometre.

Secondary — the Resident and Senior Engineers

They do not open the app. They receive its output: weekly site reports, progress tables, issue trackers, and formal queries to the contractor. What they need is that every figure be traceable to a drawing and a date.

Design implication: anything the app displays should be exportable with its provenance attached, not just its value.

A real half-day

  1. Leave the office around 07:00. Drive to an access point somewhere along the alignment.
  2. Walk a stretch — typically 1–3 km — checking drainage against the design.
  3. At each feature: identify it, judge its construction stage, photograph it, note defects.
  4. Phone in and out of signal the whole time. Frequently none at all.
  5. Back at the office: reconcile the day, write it up, flag anything needing a formal query.
The condition everyone underestimates

Harmattan dust, direct equatorial sun, and a screen touched with dusty or gloved hands. Small tap targets fail with dust on the glass. This is not a theoretical accessibility note — it is the everyday operating condition.

04The domain in ten minutes

Four concepts. Get these and the rest reads easily.

1. Chainage is the coordinate system

Everything on a railway is located by distance along the line from a datum, written PK nn+mmm.mmm — kilometres, then metres. CH 93+195 means 93,195 m from the start of the line.

This is the primary axis. Not latitude and longitude — those are an implementation detail used to work out chainage from a GPS fix. Every conversation on site, every drawing, every report, every contractor submission is indexed by chainage.

2. Left and right are defined, not relative

Left and right are taken facing the direction of increasing chainage. They do not depend on which way the engineer happens to be walking. A ditch is on the Left for the life of the project.

Almost every drainage feature is sided, the two sides carry different work, and getting a side wrong sends someone across live construction to the wrong place.

3. A drainage feature is a thing with a cross-section

The design uses a numbered type taxonomy. A sample of what a single section carries:

4. Construction happens in stages, and stages are the unit of progress

A structure is not "done" or "not done". It moves through a sequence, and each step is formally submitted by the contractor as an Inspection Request that the consultant approves or rejects:

Not started → Excavation → Blinding → Rebar → Shuttered → Concreted → Completed and approved

Most of what the engineer records is "which stage is this at, and does it match what the contractor claims". On one section, 57% of cross-drainage structures are part-built — started, then left, some for more than two years. Stage is the interesting state, not completion.

05How drainage is positioned

The geometry you are representing. This is why the drawings are in this brief.

A drainage feature is located by line, chainage, side and offset from the centreline. Those four together, never fewer. But the features themselves are far from uniform, and the variety is the design problem.

Linear or point

A ditch runs — it has a start chainage and an end chainage, and lengths range from about 20 m to 1,700 m. A culvert sits — one chainage, crossing under the track. A water descent is a point on a slope. A riprap callout marks a stretch of armouring. Points outnumber linear features roughly four to one by count, but linear features dominate what the eye follows.

Offset — features live at different distances from the track

Transverse position is as real as chainage, and it stacks:

RoughlyWhat is there
0 mTrack centreline. Buried collector drains run beneath the platform at stations.
~3.5 mEdge of formation — platform side ditches, half-round ditches.
~10–16 mFoot of slope — the main longitudinal ditches, Types 4, 7 and 12.
~24 mCrest of cutting — crest ditches, where they exist.
50–200 m+Off-alignment — diversion channels redirecting a watercourse, discharging away from the railway.

Features coexist and overlap

  • At one chainage there can be a buried collector drain, a platform side ditch, a foot-of-slope ditch and riprap — all on the same side, at different offsets, all separate items of work.
  • The two sides commonly carry different types over the same stretch, with different extents.
  • A ditch run frequently changes type partway — Type 7 for 200 m, then Type 12 through a culvert approach, then Type 7 again. The change is a real transition an engineer has to find on the ground.
  • Long stretches deliberately have no longitudinal ditch at all, where the ground is too flat to grade one. Absence is a design decision, not missing data, and the design says so explicitly.

Three different kinds of positional claim

This distinction is invisible in the source data and matters enormously on site:

  • A quoted chainage — a culvert at CH=111+017.229. Exact, written on the drawing.
  • A derived extent — a ditch run read from the spacing of symbology markers set at 7.6–8.0 m centres. Good to about ±4 m and no better.
  • A label position — a riprap callout marks where the annotation sits, not where the work starts and stops. The extent is stated in the text, not in the geometry.

06How the drawings represent it

A century of railway engineering has already solved this representation problem once, on paper. Worth understanding what it does before deciding what to do on a screen.

The design is issued as A1 sheets at 1:1000. Each sheet carries two horizontal strips of about 700 m of railway, one above the other, each with a station band beneath it giving position. The corridor is cut into slices and stacked.

Type is encoded, not written

The thing that makes this domain unusual: ditch type does not appear as text on the plans. It is carried by line style, marker glyph and colour. A black × along a line means Type 7. A blue × means Type 4. A black ⁄ means Type 12. Red invert levels with a gradient mean Type 1. The legend is the entire key.

What a stretch actually looks like

What the paper convention gets right, and what it costs

It works because

  • Position along the line is always readable — the station band never leaves.
  • Sidedness is spatial and immediate: above the line or below it.
  • Offset is to scale, so the relationship between platform ditch, toe ditch and crest ditch is visible at a glance.
  • Overlapping features separate naturally because they sit at different offsets.
  • Absence is visible — a stretch with no markers plainly has no ditch.

It costs

  • Type requires memorising a legend of near-identical glyphs.
  • Anything longer than 700 m is split across strips, and anything longer than 1,400 m across sheets.
  • Transverse scale and longitudinal scale are the same, so a 40 m-wide corridor drawn over 700 m is a very thin band of useful information.
  • Nothing shows construction state. That is the app’s entire addition.
  • It is a metre wide and unusable in wind.

07The representation problem

The central design question, stated plainly. Everything else in this brief is context for this section.

How do you represent a corridor 380 km long and 40 m wide, carrying overlapping sided features of wildly different extents and three different kinds of positional certainty, on a phone held in one hand in direct sunlight?

The specific tensions:

TensionWhat makes it hard
Aspect ratioTen thousand to one. At a zoom where the corridor fits, features are invisible. At a zoom where features are legible, you see a few hundred metres of a 380 km project and lose all sense of where you are.
Density is unevenMedian 18 features per kilometre, peaking above 40. Open line is sparse; a station or a stream crossing is dense. Whatever handles the sparse case must not collapse in the dense one.
SidednessInherently spatial — but the most useful ordering is usually linear, by distance ahead. Those two wants pull in opposite directions.
Offset stackingFour features at one chainage on one side, at different distances from the track, are four separate items of work. Flattening them to a single point loses real information.
Extent versus pointA 1,700 m ditch run and a single culvert are both "a feature". One is a span, one is a position, and a third kind is a label whose true extent lives in its text.
Certainty is invisibleA quoted culvert chainage is exact. A derived ditch extent is ±4 m. Nothing in the source distinguishes them, and an engineer setting out from the wrong one makes an expensive mistake.
Plan versus sectionWhere a ditch runs is a plan question. What it is — the shape an engineer has to recognise in the ground — is a section question. Both are needed, and the drawings keep them on separate sheets.
Off-alignment featuresA diversion channel 200 m away serves a chainage on the line. It belongs to that chainage but is not near it.
Absence is dataLong stretches deliberately have no ditch. The design says why. A display that simply shows nothing there is indistinguishable from missing data.

None of these has an obvious answer, and the paper convention only solves some of them. Finding an answer that holds on a phone is the work.

09The jobs

Eight things the engineer needs to do, ordered by how often they happen.

Orient

“What chainage am I at?”

  1. Open the app while walking
  2. Read position without stopping
  3. Know how far that reading can be trusted
Look ahead

“What is coming in the next 200 m?”

  1. Scan forward along the line
  2. See type, side and stage at a glance
  3. Decide what to walk to next
Identify

“What is this thing in front of me?”

  1. Point at something physical
  2. Match it to a design feature
  3. Confirm type, side, offset, dimensions and drawing
Record a stage

“This culvert is at shuttering.”

  1. Select the feature
  2. Set the construction stage
  3. Save in seconds, one-handed, without stopping
Log a defect

“This ditch is undermined.”

  1. Flag the feature
  2. Write an observation
  3. Attach photographs
  4. Have it appear in the office as an open item
Check the claim

“They say this is complete.”

  1. See the contractor’s submitted stage and its date
  2. Compare with what is in front of him
  3. Record agreement or disagreement
Find a gap

“Where has nothing been built?”

  1. Scan a long stretch
  2. Spot un-started or stalled work
  3. See how long it has been stalled
Close the day

“What did I cover?”

  1. Review everything recorded
  2. Confirm it is safely stored
  3. Export it for the weekly report
The one to optimise for

Record a stage happens dozens of times per walk, standing up, often mid-stride, with one hand. If that interaction is not fast and unmistakable, nothing else in the design matters. Everything else can afford a tap more.

11Constraints that will bite

Ignore any one of these and the design fails on site rather than in review.

Environment

  • Direct sun, 10:00–15:00. Assume the screen is at a fraction of its usable contrast.
  • Dust. Harmattan season coats the screen. Tap targets need to be generous; precision gestures will fail.
  • One hand, often moving. Everything frequent must be thumb-reachable.
  • Heat. Phones throttle and dim. Continuous animation and heavy rendering are expensive.

Connectivity and device

  • No signal for hours at a time. Offline is the normal state, not an error state.
  • GPS accuracy varies from about 3 m to unusable. The app must always show how much to trust the position, and must remain fully usable with GPS off entirely.
  • Mid-range Android, installed as a web app. Battery matters across a four-hour walk.
  • Nine sections of data. Not all of it can be resident at once; what is carried and what is fetched is a design consideration, not only an engineering one.

Data integrity

Field data is irreplaceable

Design data can always be re-derived from the drawings. Field observations cannot — they exist only because someone stood there and looked. On this project a bulk write once overwrote genuine site observations, because sync resolves conflicts by last-write-wins.

Design consequence: recorded observations must be visibly distinct from design data, must never be silently replaced, and the user must be able to see that a record is safely stored. Any destructive action needs to be obvious and reversible.

  • Photographs keep both the raw GPS fix and the chainage they were snapped to — those are different claims and both are needed.
  • Every record carries who made it and when, because that travels into formal documents.

12Non-negotiables

Five. Everything else is open.

RuleWhy
Never present a number the data cannot supportDerived extents are good to ±4 m; quoted callouts are exact; label positions are neither. If the design cannot distinguish them, it is inviting a setting-out error.
Design intent and site observation never merge“The drawing says Type 12” and “I saw a Type 12” are different claims, and the second is what gets certified.
Position is line, chainage and side — never chainage aloneTwo lines, nine sub-sections, and sided features throughout. Chainage on its own is ambiguous.
Offline is the default stateNot a degraded mode, not a banner. The app behaves the same with or without signal; sync is a background fact the user can check, not something they manage.
No satellite or aerial imageryStated user requirement. The subject is the engineered corridor — centreline, earthworks boundary, ditch runs, structures — and photographic imagery buries all of it.

13Undecided, deliberately

This brief takes no position on any of the following. They are the design.

  • The primary representation of the corridor, and whether a map exists at all.
  • How position along a 380 km line is navigated and displayed.
  • How sidedness and offset are expressed.
  • How overlapping features at one chainage are separated.
  • How the three kinds of positional certainty are distinguished.
  • How construction stage is set — the interaction, not just the control.
  • How design intent is held apart from site observation, visually and structurally.
  • How nine sub-sections are moved between.
  • Information architecture: screens, modes, or one continuous surface.
  • The entire visual language — palette, typography, density, iconography, light and dark.
  • What the app looks like when it has nothing to show, and when it has far too much.

No colour, type or layout direction is given anywhere in this brief, and none should be inferred from the drawing extracts — those are here to explain the domain, not to suggest a style.

14What to deliver

In the order that unblocks the most.

  1. A point of view — a page on how you propose to represent the corridor and why, before any pixels. Section 07 is the question; this is your answer to it. Everything else follows.
  2. The core loop — orient, scan ahead, identify, record a stage. Phone width. This is most of the app’s use.
  3. Feature detail in full — design data with certainty expressed, stage control, notes, photographs, defect flag, and the contractor’s claim history. Show a dense case: a structure with a dozen inspection requests and an open defect.
  4. The corridor view — whatever your point of view proposes.
  5. Moving between sub-sections — nine of them, unevenly complete.
  6. States — no GPS; poor GPS; offline with unsaved records; a chainage with 40+ features; a chainage with none, where absence is intentional; a feature never inspected; a feature whose drawing is not approved for construction.
  7. A design system — tokens, type scale, category treatment, stage treatment, components, touch target sizes.

How to judge it

  • Can a stage be recorded in seconds, one-handed, without stopping walking?
  • Is the current position readable at arm’s length in direct sun?
  • Can the user tell a ±4 m derived extent from an exact quoted chainage from a label position?
  • Can they tell design intent from their own observation without reading labels?
  • Does a stretch with 41 features in 400 m still read clearly — and does an empty stretch read as intentional rather than broken?
  • Does it hold up at 380 km and at 40 m?
  • Does the app behave identically with the network off?
